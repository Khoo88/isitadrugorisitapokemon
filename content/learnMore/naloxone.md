## What is Naloxone?

Naloxone is a competitive opioid antagonist with high affinity for mu-opioid receptors. It displaces opioids from receptors, rapidly reversing respiratory depression, sedation, and hypotension in opioid overdose. It has no agonist activity and does not produce euphoria or dependence.

Intranasal, intramuscular, and intravenous formulations are available for community and emergency use. Duration of action (30–90 minutes for many formulations) may be shorter than long-acting opioids (methadone, fentanyl analogs), requiring repeat dosing and emergency medical care.

It precipitates acute withdrawal in opioid-dependent individuals—uncomfortable but not an indication to withhold treatment in life-threatening overdose.

## Primary Uses

- Emergency treatment of known or suspected opioid overdose (respiratory depression, unresponsiveness)
- Community distribution and co-prescription programs for patients on chronic opioids
- Postoperative opioid reversal (anesthesia, titrated carefully to avoid pain crisis)
- Neonatal opioid withdrawal not treated with naloxone routinely (specialist protocols differ)

Universal public health recommendation for naloxone access for at-risk individuals and households.

## Common Side Effects

- Precipitated opioid withdrawal (agitation, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, tachycardia, hypertension, pain)
- Pulmonary edema (rare, high-dose rapid IV)
- Hypertension, arrhythmia (withdrawal-related)
- **Warnings:** Does not reverse benzodiazepine or stimulant overdose alone; seek emergency care after administration; repeat doses may be needed; partial agonists (buprenorphine) may require higher or repeated doses.
